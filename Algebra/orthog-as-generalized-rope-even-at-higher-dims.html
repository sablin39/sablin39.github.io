A quick review of RoPE and orthogonal matrices

The relative-position law

Standard RoPE rotates each pair of query and key features through an angle ωx\omega xωx. Here x∈Rx\in\mathbb{R}x∈R is the scalar position and ω∈R\omega\in\mathbb{R}ω∈R is the frequency assigned to that pair. The full encoding matrix arranges these rotations in diagonal blocks, as in 苏剑林. (Mar. 23, 2021). 《Transformer升级之路：2、博采众长的旋转式位置编码 》.

Let Rx∈Rn×n\mathcal{R}_x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}Rx​∈Rn×n be an orthogonal encoding matrix at position xxx. Orthogonality gives RxTRx=In\mathcal{R}_x^T\mathcal{R}_x=I_nRxT​Rx​=In​, where InI_nIn​ is the n×nn\times nn×n identity, so the encoding preserves vector lengths. We recover the relative-position behavior of RoPE by requiring

Setting y=xy=xy=x gives R0=In\mathcal{R}_0=I_nR0​=In​. The group of orthogonal transformations

The real n×nn\times nn×n orthogonal matrices form the group O(n)O(n)O(n) under multiplication. Its identity is InI_nIn​. For R∈O(n)R\in O(n)R∈O(n), the inverse is RTR^TRT, and RTR=InR^TR=I_nRTR=In​ gives det⁡R=±1\det R=\pm1detR=±1. The determinant-+1+1+1 component is the rotation subgroup SO(n)SO(n)SO(n). The determinant-−1-1−1 component consists of orientation-reversing transformations. Composing two such transformations gives determinant +1+1+1 and lands back in SO(n)SO(n)SO(n).

Every eigenvalue λ∈C\lambda\in\mathbb{C}λ∈C of a real orthogonal matrix RRR has modulus one. A nonreal eigenvalue and its conjugate contribute λλ‾=1\lambda\overline{\lambda}=1λλ=1 to the determinant. The only possible real eigenvalues are +1+1+1 and −1-1−1, so det⁡R=(−1)r−\det R=(-1)^{r_-}detR=(−1)r−​, where r−r_-r−​ counts the eigenvalues equal to −1-1−1, with multiplicity. An even count puts RRR in SO(n)SO(n)SO(n), and an odd count puts it in the orientation-reversing component of O(n)O(n)O(n).

In the plane, diag⁡(−1,1)\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)diag(−1,1) reflects across the second coordinate axis and has determinant −1-1−1. The half-turn diag⁡(−1,−1)\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1)diag(−1,−1) reverses both coordinates. As the rotation angle goes from zero to π\piπ, the eigenvalues move around the unit circle as a conjugate pair and arrive at −1-1−1 together, with product +1+1+1 throughout.

For RoPE, the smooth family starts at R0=In\mathcal{R}_0=I_nR0​=In​, where the determinant is +1+1+1. Since det⁡Rx\det\mathcal{R}_xdetRx​ is continuous in xxx and takes values in {+1,−1}\{+1,-1\}{+1,−1}, every encoding matrix Rx\mathcal{R}_xRx​ has determinant +1+1+1 and belongs to SO(n)SO(n)SO(n).

With the addition law, R:(R,+)→SO(n)\mathcal{R}:(\mathbb{R},+)\to SO(n)R:(R,+)→SO(n) is a group homomorphism.

The Lie algebra describes the infinitesimal rotations at the identity. Let Q(t)∈SO(n)Q(t)\in SO(n)Q(t)∈SO(n) be a smooth matrix curve with real parameter ttt and Q(0)=InQ(0)=I_nQ(0)=In​. Generalizing RoPE to vector positions

Why a direct quaternion generalization fails

Write a two-dimensional position as (ξ,η)∈R2(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^2(ξ,η)∈R2, with real coordinates ξ\xiξ and η\etaη. Let i,j,k\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}i,j,k be the imaginary basis units of the quaternions H\mathbb{H}H. The planar unit complex number eiθe^{i\theta}eiθ suggests the unit quaternion ρ(ξ,η)=eξi+ηj∈H\rho(\xi,\eta)=e^{\xi\mathbf{i}+\eta\mathbf{j}}\in\mathbb{H}ρ(ξ,η)=eξi+ηj∈H considered in 苏剑林. (May. 10, 2021). 《Transformer升级之路：4、二维位置的旋转式位置编码 》.

For a pure imaginary quaternion vvv, the quaternion exponential is

ev=cos⁡∣v∣+v∣v∣sin⁡∣v∣, e^v=\cos|v|+\frac{v}{|v|}\sin|v|, ev=cos∣v∣+∣v∣v​sin∣v∣,

with e0=1e^0=1e0=1. For pure imaginary quaternions a,ba,ba,b, multiplication splits into scalar and vector parts:

ab=−a⋅b+a×b. ab=-a\cdot b+a\times b. ab=−a⋅b+a×b.

In particular, ab−ba=2a×bab-ba=2a\times bab−ba=2a×b. These formulas make the composition calculation below explicit rather than an appeal to a special quaternion identity.

Left multiplication by this unit quaternion preserves length in the four real components, so it acts on the feature vector as an orthogonal 4×44\times44×4 matrix. The question is whether these transformations compose according to position addition.

The steps (ξ,0)(\xi,0)(ξ,0) and (0,η)(0,\eta)(0,η) add to the same position in either order. For the quaternion encoding to preserve relative positions, both products would have to give ρ(ξ,η)\rho(\xi,\eta)ρ(ξ,η). But ij=k\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}ij=k and ji=−k\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}=-\mathbf{k}ji=−k, so expanding the exponentials gives

eξieηj=(cos⁡ξ+isin⁡ξ)(cos⁡η+jsin⁡η)=cos⁡ξcos⁡η+isin⁡ξcos⁡η+jcos⁡ξsin⁡η+ksin⁡ξsin⁡η,eηjeξi=cos⁡ξcos⁡η+isin⁡ξcos⁡η+jcos⁡ξsin⁡η−ksin⁡ξsin⁡η. \begin{aligned} e^{\xi\mathbf{i}}e^{\eta\mathbf{j}} &=(\cos\xi+\mathbf i\sin\xi)(\cos\eta+\mathbf j\sin\eta)\\ &=\cos\xi\cos\eta+\mathbf i\sin\xi\cos\eta +\mathbf j\cos\xi\sin\eta+\mathbf k\sin\xi\sin\eta,\\ e^{\eta\mathbf{j}}e^{\xi\mathbf{i}} &=\cos\xi\cos\eta+\mathbf i\sin\xi\cos\eta +\mathbf j\cos\xi\sin\eta-\mathbf k\sin\xi\sin\eta. \end{aligned} eξieηjeηjeξi​=(cosξ+isinξ)(cosη+jsinη)=cosξcosη+isinξcosη+jcosξsinη+ksinξsinη,=cosξcosη+isinξcosη+jcosξsinη−ksinξsinη.​

Subtracting them yields

eξieηj−eηjeξi=2sin⁡ξsin⁡η k. e^{\xi\mathbf{i}}e^{\eta\mathbf{j}}-e^{\eta\mathbf{j}}e^{\xi\mathbf{i}} =2\sin\xi\sin\eta\,\mathbf{k}. eξieηj−eηjeξi=2sinξsinηk.

For general ξ\xiξ and η\etaη, the order changes the result, so the encoding fails the relative-position law.

The same limitation applies to any smooth encoding that uses multiplication of unit quaternions to satisfy the relative-position law. At the identity, its coordinate generators are pure imaginary because differentiating ρρ‾=1\rho\overline{\rho}=1ρρ​=1 gives a+aˉ=0a+\bar a=0a+aˉ=0. For two pure imaginary quaternion generators a,b∈Ha,b\in\mathbb{H}a,b∈H, the infinitesimal difference between the two composition orders is governed by ab−ba=2a×bab-ba=2a\times bab−ba=2a×b, where the cross product identifies aaa and bbb with their vectors in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3. The cross product vanishes only when the generators are linearly dependent. All the coordinate generators therefore lie along one imaginary axis, giving at most one independent direction.

Axial RoPE

The quaternion construction fails because rotations associated with independent position coordinates do not compose consistently. We therefore need coordinate rotations that commute. The simplest repair assigns each coordinate to its own feature plane, which is exactly the coordinate-aligned construction used by axial RoPE.

Let ξ\xiξ rotate the first pair of real features and η\etaη rotate the second. Each planar rotation belongs to SO(2)SO(2)SO(2), so the precise group inclusion is

R(ξ,η)=diag⁡ ⁣(eξK,eηK)∈SO(2)×SO(2)⊂SO(4). \mathcal{R}_{(\xi,\eta)} =\operatorname{diag}\!\left(e^{\xi K},e^{\eta K}\right) \in SO(2)\times SO(2)\subset SO(4). R(ξ,η)​=diag(eξK,eηK)∈SO(2)×SO(2)⊂SO(4).

Thus R(ξ,η)\mathcal R_{(\xi,\eta)}R(ξ,η)​ is a rotation in SO(4)SO(4)SO(4), but it is not an arbitrary four-dimensional rotation: it uses only two independent planar rotations. This forms a two-dimensional subgroup of the six-dimensional group SO(4)SO(4)SO(4). See maximal torus for the general group-theoretic description of this subgroup.

The distinction becomes explicit by writing the coordinate generators, which are infinitesimal rotations rather than the position-dependent rotation itself:

Jξ=(K000),Jη=(000K),Jξ,Jη∈so(4). J_\xi= \begin{pmatrix} K&0\\ 0&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J_\eta= \begin{pmatrix} 0&0\\ 0&K \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J_\xi,J_\eta\in\mathfrak{so}(4). Jξ​=(K0​00​),Jη​=(00​0K​),Jξ​,Jη​∈so(4).

Here so(4)\mathfrak{so}(4)so(4) is the Lie algebra of real skew-symmetric matrices, whereas SO(4)SO(4)SO(4) is the group of determinant-one orthogonal matrices. Exponentiating an infinitesimal generator produces an actual rotation:

R(ξ,η)=eξJξ+ηJη=eξJξeηJη=eηJηeξJξ∈SO(2)×SO(2). \mathcal R_{(\xi,\eta)} =e^{\xi J_\xi+\eta J_\eta} =e^{\xi J_\xi}e^{\eta J_\eta} =e^{\eta J_\eta}e^{\xi J_\xi} \in SO(2)\times SO(2). R(ξ,η)​=eξJξ​+ηJη​=eξJξ​eηJη​=eηJη​eξJξ​∈SO(2)×SO(2).

For a matrix Lie algebra, the Lie bracket is the matrix commutator [A,B]=AB−BA[A,B]=AB-BA[A,B]=AB−BA. Here AAA and BBB are skew-symmetric infinitesimal generators. Since JξJ_\xiJξ​ and JηJ_\etaJη​ act on disjoint feature planes, each product vanishes blockwise:

[Jξ,Jη]=JξJη−JηJξ=0−0=0. [J_\xi,J_\eta] =J_\xi J_\eta-J_\eta J_\xi =0-0=0. [Jξ​,Jη​]=Jξ​Jη​−Jη​Jξ​=0−0=0.

It is this vanishing commutator that lets the exponentials compose in either order, not orthogonality alone.

Axial RoPE assigns each feature pair to one position axis, with a frequency for that pair. Qwen2-VL’s multimodal RoPE (M-RoPE) uses this idea for time, height, and width.1 Qwen3-VL interleaves the axis assignments across frequency bands, with each rotary pair still using one coordinate.2 Cosmos 3 likewise divides the rotary channels of each attention head among temporal, height, and width coordinates. 3

This coordinate-aligned construction is only one way to satisfy the commuting-generator condition. The next step is to isolate that condition and derive the general encoding without requiring the feature planes to align with coordinate axes.

Equality therefore requires [Ji,Jj]=JiJj−JjJi=0[J_i,J_j]=J_iJ_j-J_jJ_i=0[Ji​,Jj​]=Ji​Jj​−Jj​Ji​=0. This is the condition on the generators imposed by the composition law. Since x=∑ixiei\mathsf{x}=\sum_i x_i e_ix=∑i​xi​ei​, the same law reconstructs the full encoding:

Rx=∏i=1pRxiei=∏i=1pexiJi=exp⁡ ⁣(∑i=1pxiJi). \mathcal R_{\mathsf x} =\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathcal R_{x_ie_i} =\prod_{i=1}^{p}e^{x_iJ_i} =\exp\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_iJ_i\right). Rx​=i=1∏p​Rxi​ei​​=i=1∏p​exi​Ji​=exp(i=1∑p​xi​Ji​).

Conversely, pairwise commuting skew-symmetric generators give an orthogonal encoding with the required composition law. For the matrix A=∑ixiJi∈so(n)A=\sum_i x_iJ_i\in\mathfrak{so}(n)A=∑i​xi​Ji​∈so(n), skew-symmetry and (eA)T=eAT(e^A)^T=e^{A^T}(eA)T=eAT give (eA)TeA=eATeA=e−AeA=In(e^A)^Te^A=e^{A^T}e^A=e^{-A}e^A=I_n(eA)TeA=eATeA=e−AeA=In​. Since any two such exponents commute, their exponentials compose by adding the exponents.

The geometric statement is that (Rp,+)(\mathbb R^p,+)(Rp,+) is abelian, so the image of R\mathcal RR must also be abelian. The coordinate generators therefore commute, and the whole encoding lies in a common commuting family of rotations. The next step is to identify that family’s shared rotation planes.

From coordinate generators to a commuting subalgebra

The relative-position law constrains not only each individual JiJ_iJi​, but every infinitesimal rotation available through the encoding. Collect all real linear combinations of the coordinate generators into

h=span⁡R{J1,…,Jp}={∑i=1paiJi  |  a1,…,ap∈R}⊆so(n). \mathfrak h=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{J_1,\ldots,J_p\} =\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_iJ_i\;\middle|\;a_1,\ldots,a_p\in\mathbb R\right\} \subseteq\mathfrak{so}(n). h=spanR​{J1​,…,Jp​}={i=1∑p​ai​Ji​​a1​,…,ap​∈R}⊆so(n).

Every nonzero A∈hA\in\mathfrak hA∈h describes an allowed direction of infinitesimal rotation. Because every pair of coordinate generators commutes, h\mathfrak hh is an abelian subalgebra: its bracket vanishes identically. It need not contain all infinitesimal rotations available in so(n)\mathfrak{so}(n)so(n); it contains only those spanned by the position coordinates.

If some coordinate generators are linearly dependent, their number ppp need not equal dim⁡h\dim\mathfrak hdimh. For example, J2=2J1J_2=2J_1J2​=2J1​ would let two coordinates select different frequencies along the same rotation direction without adding another independent direction to h\mathfrak hh.

Common planes for commuting generators

Let a⊆so(n)\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak{so}(n)a⊆so(n) be any abelian subalgebra of infinitesimal rotations. We derive its real rotation planes by induction on dimension, using complex coordinates only to find one invariant plane at a time. No diagonalizability or simultaneous-diagonalization theorem is assumed.

Every complex square matrix has an eigenvalue because its characteristic polynomial has a complex root by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. For B∈aB\in\mathfrak aB∈a, if Bz=λzBz=\lambda zBz=λz with z≠0z\ne0z=0, skew-symmetry gives

λz∗z=z∗Bz=−(Bz)∗z=−λˉz∗z, \lambda z^*z=z^*Bz=-(Bz)^*z=-\bar\lambda z^*z, λz∗z=z∗Bz=−(Bz)∗z=−λˉz∗z,

so λ=iθ\lambda=i\thetaλ=iθ for some real θ\thetaθ. This uses only the definition of an eigenvalue, not an eigenbasis.

A nonzero skew-symmetric matrix A1=(aij)A_1=(a_{ij})A1​=(aij​) has a nonzero eigenvalue. Otherwise its characteristic polynomial would be λn\lambda^nλn. But the coefficient of λn−2\lambda^{n-2}λn−2 in det⁡(λI−A1)\det(\lambda I-A_1)det(λI−A1​) is

∑i<jaij2>0, \sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^2>0, i<j∑​aij2​>0,

obtained by taking λ\lambdaλ from all but two diagonal entries and evaluating the remaining 2×22\times22×2 minor. Since aji=−aija_{ji}=-a_{ij}aji​=−aij​, that minor contributes aij2a_{ij}^2aij2​.

If a=0\mathfrak a=0a=0, every subspace is a common kernel, handled by the pairing step below. Otherwise, choose a finite basis {A1,…,Ar}\{A_1,\ldots,A_r\}{A1​,…,Ar​} of a\mathfrak aa with A1≠0A_1\ne0A1​=0. Start with a nonzero eigenvalue iθ1i\theta_1iθ1​ of A1A_1A1​ and its eigenspace. A(Bv)=B(Av)=λ(Bv). A(Bv)=B(Av)=\lambda(Bv). A(Bv)=B(Av)=λ(Bv).

Select an eigenvector of the next matrix within that invariant complex subspace. After at most rrr restrictions, we obtain one nonzero vector zzz such that

Bz=iθ(B)zfor every B∈a, Bz=i\theta(B)z\qquad\text{for every }B\in\mathfrak a, Bz=iθ(B)zfor every B∈a,

where each θ(B)\theta(B)θ(B) is real. Linearity extends the eigenvalue relations from the basis to all of a\mathfrak aa. This constructs one common eigenvector without assuming a common eigenbasis.

Write z=u−ivz=u-ivz=u−iv with real vectors u,vu,vu,v. To see that they are linearly independent, suppose au+bv=0au+bv=0au+bv=0. Since θ1≠0\theta_1\ne0θ1​=0, applying A1A_1A1​ gives

av−bu=0. av-bu=0. av−bu=0.

Thus W=span⁡R{u,v}W=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{u,v\}W=spanR​{u,v} is two-dimensional. Separating real and imaginary parts in Bz=iθ(B)zBz=i\theta(B)zBz=iθ(B)z gives

Bu=θ(B)v,Bv=−θ(B)u, Bu=\theta(B)v,\qquad Bv=-\theta(B)u, Bu=θ(B)v,Bv=−θ(B)u,

so WWW is invariant under every B∈aB\in\mathfrak aB∈a. In particular, applying skew-symmetry to A1A_1A1​ gives

0=uTA1u=θ1uTv,uTA1v=−θ1∥u∥2=−θ1∥v∥2. 0=u^TA_1u=\theta_1u^Tv,\qquad u^TA_1v=-\theta_1\|u\|^2=-\theta_1\|v\|^2. 0=uTA1​u=θ1​uTv,uTA1​v=−θ1​∥u∥2=−θ1​∥v∥2.

Since θ1≠0\theta_1\ne0θ1​=0, uuu and vvv are orthogonal and have the same nonzero norm. Gram–Schmidt in the order (u,v)(u,v)(u,v) therefore gives the orthonormal basis

u^=u∥u∥,v^=v∥v∥. \hat u=\frac{u}{\|u\|},\qquad\hat v=\frac{v}{\|v\|}. u^=∥u∥u​,v^=∥v∥v​.

Every restriction B∣WB|_WB∣W​ remains skew-symmetric, and in this ordered basis it acts as θ(B)K\theta(B)Kθ(B)K, without an additional sign or scale factor.

The orthogonal complement W⊥W^\perpW⊥ is also invariant under the whole family. If x∈W⊥x\in W^\perpx∈W⊥ and w∈Ww\in Ww∈W, then Bw∈WBw\in WBw∈W and

wT(Bx)=−(Bw)Tx=0. w^T(Bx)=-(Bw)^Tx=0. wT(Bx)=−(Bw)Tx=0.

Repeat the argument on the restrictions to W⊥W^\perpW⊥: these restrictions remain skew-symmetric and pairwise commuting. If every basis matrix vanishes there, that remaining subspace is a common kernel; otherwise choose a nonzero basis matrix and construct another invariant plane. Each step reduces the dimension by two. Finally, pair directions in the common kernel into zero-rotation planes, leaving at most one unpaired direction.

The orthonormal bases of all these subspaces form the columns of an orthogonal matrix PPP. With m=⌊n/2⌋m=\lfloor n/2\rfloorm=⌊n/2⌋, the induction therefore proves the common-plane decomposition:

A=P diag⁡ ⁣(θ1(A)K,…,θm(A)K,[0])PTfor every A∈a. A=P\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left( \theta_1(A)K,\ldots,\theta_m(A)K,[0] \right)P^T \qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathfrak a. A=Pdiag(θ1​(A)K,…,θm​(A)K,[0])PTfor every A∈a.

The final scalar block appears only when nnn is odd. Each coefficient θℓ(A)\theta_\ell(A)θℓ​(A) is real and linear in AAA because its block varies linearly with AAA.

This gives an immediate upper bound on the dimension of a\mathfrak aa. Define

Φ:a⟶Rm,Φ(A)=(θ1(A)⋯θm(A))T. \Phi:\mathfrak a\longrightarrow\mathbb R^m, \qquad \Phi(A)= \begin{pmatrix} \theta_1(A)&\cdots&\theta_m(A) \end{pmatrix}^{T}. Φ:a⟶Rm,Φ(A)=(θ1​(A)​⋯​θm​(A)​)T.

The map Φ\PhiΦ is linear, and it is injective because Φ(A)=0\Phi(A)=0Φ(A)=0 makes every block in the decomposition zero, hence A=0A=0A=0. Therefore,

dim⁡a≤m. \dim\mathfrak a\le m. dima≤m.

Lie algebra rank from the common-plane decomposition

The standard coordinate-aligned family already realizes mmm independent planes:

hstd={diag⁡(a1K,…,amK,[0]):a1,…,am∈R}. \mathfrak h_{\mathrm{std}} = \left\{ \operatorname{diag}(a_1K,\ldots,a_mK,[0]) :a_1,\ldots,a_m\in\mathbb R \right\}. hstd​={diag(a1​K,…,am​K,[0]):a1​,…,am​∈R}.

Its members commute, and its dimension is mmm. Since every abelian subspace has dimension at most mmm, no abelian subspace properly contains hstd\mathfrak h_{\mathrm{std}}hstd​. It is therefore a maximal abelian subalgebra of so(n)\mathfrak{so}(n)so(n).

For the compact Lie algebra so(n)\mathfrak{so}(n)so(n), such a maximal abelian subalgebra of infinitesimal rotations is a Cartan subalgebra, and its dimension is the rank of the Lie algebra. Hence

rank⁡so(n)=m=⌊n2⌋. \boxed{\operatorname{rank}\mathfrak{so}(n) =m=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor.} rankso(n)=m=⌊2n​⌋.​

This rank counts the maximum number of independent infinitesimal rotation planes available to a commuting encoding. It is neither the dimension of so(n)\mathfrak{so}(n)so(n) nor the matrix rank of any individual generator. In particular, so(3)\mathfrak{so}(3)so(3) has rank one, while so(4)\mathfrak{so}(4)so(4) has rank two.

The position-angle map and its rank

For a single position coordinate, the planar rotation blocks follow from the decomposition of one skew-symmetric generator, as shown in 苏剑林. (Dec. 28, 2022). 《Transformer升级之路：6、旋转位置编码的完备性分析 》.

Apply the common-plane decomposition to the subalgebra generated by the coordinate derivatives,

h=span⁡R{J1,…,Jp}. \mathfrak h=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{J_1,\ldots,J_p\}. h=spanR​{J1​,…,Jp​}.

Each coefficient functional θℓ\theta_\ellθℓ​ is determined by its values on the coordinate generators. Define ωℓi=θℓ(Ji)\omega_{\ell i}=\theta_\ell(J_i)ωℓi​=θℓ​(Ji​) and let

Ω=(ωℓi)∈Rm×p. \Omega=(\omega_{\ell i})\in\mathbb R^{m\times p}. Ω=(ωℓi​)∈Rm×p.

For x=(x1,…,xp)\mathsf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_p)x=(x1​,…,xp​), the combined generator is Ax=∑ixiJiA_{\mathsf x}=\sum_i x_iJ_iAx​=∑i​xi​Ji​, so the angle in plane ℓ\ellℓ is

θℓ(Ax)=∑i=1pωℓixi. \theta_\ell(A_{\mathsf x}) =\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega_{\ell i}x_i. θℓ​(Ax​)=i=1∑p​ωℓi​xi​.

The encoding therefore takes the block-rotation form

Rx=P diag⁡ ⁣(eθ1(Ax)K,…,eθm(Ax)K,[1])PT, \mathcal R_{\mathsf x} =P\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left( e^{\theta_1(A_{\mathsf x})K},\ldots, e^{\theta_m(A_{\mathsf x})K},[1] \right)P^T, Rx​=Pdiag(eθ1​(Ax​)K,…,eθm​(Ax​)K,[1])PT,

where the final scalar block [1][1][1] appears only when nnn is odd.

A displacement v∈Rp\mathsf v\in\mathbb R^pv∈Rp leaves every rotation angle unchanged exactly when Ωv=0\Omega\mathsf v=0Ωv=0. Consequently, the number of independent position directions is rank⁡Ω\operatorname{rank}\OmegarankΩ. Since Ω∈Rm×p\Omega\in\mathbb R^{m\times p}Ω∈Rm×p,

0≤rank⁡Ω≤min⁡(p,m). 0\le\operatorname{rank}\Omega\le\min(p,m). 0≤rankΩ≤min(p,m).

The lower endpoint rank⁡Ω=0\operatorname{rank}\Omega=0rankΩ=0 means that every rotation angle vanishes, so the encoding is the identity matrix at every position and carries no positional information. Excluding that degenerate case gives

1≤rank⁡Ω≤min⁡ ⁣(p,⌊n2⌋). \boxed{1\le\operatorname{rank}\Omega \le\min\!\left(p,\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor\right).} 1≤rankΩ≤min(p,⌊2n​⌋).​

The lower bound agrees with Su’s one-dimensional construction: if J≠0J\ne0J=0, then JJJ has a nonzero conjugate eigenvalue pair ±iω\pm i\omega±iω, so at least one real plane undergoes a nontrivial rotation. The upper bound requires the commuting-family argument above. Assigning an independent coordinate to each plane, as axial RoPE does, gives rank⁡Ω=m\operatorname{rank}\Omega=mrankΩ=m when p≥mp\ge mp≥m and attains the feature-space bound.

The columns of Ω\OmegaΩ are Φ(Ji)\Phi(J_i)Φ(Ji​), so Ωx=Φ(Ax)\Omega\mathsf x=\Phi(A_{\mathsf x})Ωx=Φ(Ax​) and

rank⁡Ω=dim⁡Φ(h)=dim⁡h, \operatorname{rank}\Omega =\dim\Phi(\mathfrak h) =\dim\mathfrak h, rankΩ=dimΦ(h)=dimh,

because Φ\PhiΦ is injective on h\mathfrak hh. This equality does not imply that every available plane receives an independent position direction. Different coordinate generators may control several planes through the same position-angle direction, so rank⁡Ω\operatorname{rank}\OmegarankΩ can be smaller than the number mmm of available commuting planes even though rank⁡Ω=dim⁡h\operatorname{rank}\Omega=\dim\mathfrak hrankΩ=dimh.

For example, take J=diag⁡(K,K)J=\operatorname{diag}(K,K)J=diag(K,K) in four feature dimensions. The two planes share the same generator and therefore the same position-angle direction. Here m=2m=2m=2, while h=span⁡{J}\mathfrak h=\operatorname{span}\{J\}h=span{J} and rank⁡Ω=1\operatorname{rank}\Omega=1rankΩ=1. The dimension of the available commuting-plane family exceeds the number of independent position directions, without contradicting the equality rank⁡Ω=dim⁡h\operatorname{rank}\Omega=\dim\mathfrak hrankΩ=dimh.

For axial RoPE, each row of Ω\OmegaΩ uses a single coordinate. The general form allows each plane to use a linear combination of the position coordinates in the shared rotation basis.
